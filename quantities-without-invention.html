Explain quantities without inventing metrics

Say what the number means, and what you cannot prove.

Goal: Explain an observed quantity with its unit, source, comparison, and limits—or clearly say that you lack a reliable measurement.

Start the pair drillUse the worksheetCheck your attempt

Numbers can make an answer easier to understand, but a precise-looking number is not automatically good evidence. A count needs a unit and a scope. A comparison needs a baseline and a period. A percentage needs a denominator. Sometimes the honest answer is a concrete observation without a numerical result. This drill helps you choose the strongest statement your actual information supports. It is not a request to add metrics to every CV bullet or to borrow the fictional quantities below.

Worked example — explicitly fictional

Fictional practice scenario: Priya changed a weekly inventory check. In one recorded check before the change, the team found 12 mismatched item records out of 80 checked. In one check afterward, it found 6 out of 80. She says, “In those two checks, mismatches fell from 12 of 80 records to 6 of 80. That is a drop from 15 percent to 7.5 percent, or 7.5 percentage points. The relative reduction is 50 percent. We sampled only two weeks, so I cannot say the change alone caused it.” The counts, denominator, and limit belong together. If she cannot access the records, she should not present these figures as her own verified history.

What goes wrong, and the repair

Weak version: “I improved accuracy by 50 percent.” That changes a reduction in observed mismatches into a claim about overall accuracy and implies causation. Another weak version is “roughly a lot better,” which gives the listener neither evidence nor a clear limitation. When no count exists, try a narrower observation: “The reviewer no longer had to ask me to fill in the missing location field on the next two forms.” That statement is useful only if it actually happened. It describes what you observed, not what you wish you had measured. Confidential measurements can be discussed only within the disclosure rules that apply to your work.

Ten-minute pair drill

You need a partner, a timer, and paper or the worksheet below. A partner’s feedback is a comprehension check, not a language score. If practising alone, record and replay each turn, then compare it with the criteria.

  1. 0–2 minutes. A chooses one genuine quantity from memory or an authorised record. Label it exact, estimated, or unavailable. B asks for the unit and denominator before hearing the claimed result.
  2. 2–4 minutes. A explains where the quantity came from and the period it covers. B writes the calculation separately from any interpretation. A rough estimate must keep its rough label.
  3. 4–6 minutes. Use the fictional inventory example to calculate both percentages and the difference. Discuss why “percentage points” and “percent reduction” produce different numbers.
  4. 6–8 minutes. Swap roles. B tells a useful outcome for which no trustworthy metric exists. A asks for an observable event, an artefact, or a clearly stated absence of measurement.
  5. 8–10 minutes. Each person records a twenty-second answer containing one claim and one limitation. The listener repeats both. Revise if the limitation disappears in the retelling.

Self-check and answer key

The fictional first rate is 12 divided by 80, or 15 percent. The second is 6 divided by 80, or 7.5 percent. Subtracting rates gives 7.5 percentage points. Dividing the drop of 6 by the original 12 gives a 50 percent relative reduction in the observed mismatch count. Neither calculation establishes causation or long-term performance. Your own answer passes when the listener can reconstruct your unit, scope, and evidence limit. “I did not measure that” can pass this exercise when followed by a relevant true observation; an invented estimate cannot.

Next attempt: Try explaining the same evidence to a colleague who dislikes percentages. Counts may be clearer. Then try a listener who asks for the percentage calculation. Keep the underlying facts identical across both versions. If the arithmetic distracts from your contribution, state the count first and offer the method as a follow-up. Write down any number you need to verify before a real interview rather than allowing a practice guess to become a remembered fact.
