Explain a technical tradeoff in plain English

Translate the decision, including what the choice costs.

Goal: Make a technical choice understandable to a listener who knows the business problem but not your tools.

Start the pair drillUse the worksheetCheck your attempt

Plain English does not mean removing all technical detail. It means choosing details that help the listener understand the decision. A tradeoff answer needs a goal, two viable options, a constraint, and a cost you accepted. Without the cost, it can sound like a product advertisement. Without the constraint, the choice can sound arbitrary. This drill is about explaining the reasoning behind your work, not proving that one technology is always superior to another.

Worked example — explicitly fictional

Fictional practice scenario: Leo is designing an internal dashboard whose source data changes throughout the day. The team could calculate every summary when someone opens the page, or prepare a summary every ten minutes. He says, “The support team needed a page that opened reliably during their morning review. Calculating everything on demand would show newer information, but it put more work on the database each time the page opened. We chose a saved summary refreshed every ten minutes. That reduced repeated calculation, but a recent change might not appear immediately. We displayed the last refresh time and kept the live order screen available for urgent checks.” This is an illustrative design decision, not a performance result from a real system.

What goes wrong, and the repair

Weak version: “We implemented a materialized strategy for scalable performance because it was optimal.” The listener cannot tell what changed for a user, what alternatives existed, or what “optimal” means. The opposite failure removes all specificity: “We chose the fast thing.” Replace tool labels with the action they perform before introducing the precise name. An analogy can help, but it must not erase the cost. A saved photograph explains freshness; it does not prove that the system is safe, fast, or correct. If the role requires technical depth, offer it after establishing the decision in ordinary language.

Ten-minute pair drill

You need a partner, a timer, and paper or the worksheet below. A partner’s feedback is a comprehension check, not a language score. If practising alone, record and replay each turn, then compare it with the criteria.

  1. 0–2 minutes. A chooses one real technical choice and writes the user problem without product or framework names. B repeats the problem in one sentence. Resolve differences before discussing the solution.
  2. 2–4 minutes. A names two options using verbs: calculate when requested, for example, versus prepare in advance. B asks what each option gives up under the actual constraint.
  3. 4–6 minutes. A explains the decision in forty-five seconds. B marks each unexplained term and repeats the accepted downside. If B cannot name a cost, the answer needs another version.
  4. 6–8 minutes. Swap roles. B explains when the rejected option would become preferable. A changes one constraint, such as freshness or maintenance capacity, and asks whether the choice should change.
  5. 8–10 minutes. Each person gives the plain version followed by one optional technical detail. The partner decides whether that detail clarifies the decision or simply adds vocabulary.

Self-check and answer key

In the fictional dashboard, the choice is between calculating on each request and preparing a saved summary. The stated priority is dependable access during a review; the accepted limitation is delayed freshness. Showing a refresh time communicates the limitation but does not remove it. A successful explanation makes that distinction clear. Score your own answer for an understandable user problem, two real options, a deciding constraint, and an acknowledged cost. The listener should also be able to say when your choice might stop being suitable. No point is earned merely for mentioning a fashionable technology.

Next attempt: Rehearse with two audiences: a technical peer and someone outside the specialty. Ask both to explain why you made the choice. They may need different detail, but should describe the same reasoning. Avoid promising measured speed or reliability unless you can support those claims. If you participated in the decision without owning it, say that plainly. “I compared the options and recommended this approach; the lead approved it” can be more informative than claiming the entire architecture.
