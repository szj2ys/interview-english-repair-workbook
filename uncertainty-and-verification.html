Admit uncertainty and propose a useful check

Separate what you know, suspect, and would verify.

Goal: Answer a question beyond your current knowledge without bluffing, giving a specific next check and a decision it could inform.

Start the pair drillUse the worksheetCheck your attempt

There are several kinds of “I do not know.” You might not remember a fact, lack experience with a tool, or need information that the question does not supply. The repair depends on the gap. This exercise helps you name that gap and propose a proportionate verification step. It is not a trick for disguising missing experience. A useful check identifies a source, an observation, and what you would do with the result. “I would research it” is a starting intention, not a complete plan.

Worked example — explicitly fictional

Fictional practice scenario: An interviewer asks Noura whether a failed background job can safely be retried. She has not used the specific queue system. Noura answers, “I have not worked with this queue, so I do not know its retry guarantees. I would first check whether the job can create a duplicate charge or other duplicate external action. I would read the queue documentation for delivery behaviour, then inspect how the application records completed work. In a safe test environment, I would interrupt a job after its external action and observe what a retry does. Until those checks passed, I would not assume that retrying is harmless.” Her answer distinguishes unfamiliar software from a relevant concern she knows how to investigate.

What goes wrong, and the repair

Weak version: “It is definitely safe; modern systems handle that.” That substitutes confidence for evidence. Another weak version is “I would Google it and ask someone,” which does not tell the interviewer what needs to be established. A better answer can be shorter than the fictional one, but must keep its boundaries. Do not describe a proposed experiment as past work you already performed. Do not test a hypothesis on production data simply because an interview scenario mentions production. In practice, explain the safe environment and the approval or expertise you would need.

Ten-minute pair drill

You need a partner, a timer, and paper or the worksheet below. A partner’s feedback is a comprehension check, not a language score. If practising alone, record and replay each turn, then compare it with the criteria.

  1. 0–2 minutes. A asks a question just beyond B’s genuine experience. B classifies the gap: forgotten fact, unfamiliar system, missing context, or unresolved evidence. A confirms what information is available.
  2. 2–4 minutes. B states one known fact, one uncertainty, and one hypothesis. A marks any phrase that sounds more certain than the evidence allows. Remove unsupported certainty without removing useful reasoning.
  3. 4–6 minutes. B proposes the smallest useful check. Name the source or environment, the observation to seek, and the decision that would change. A asks what result would contradict the hypothesis.
  4. 6–8 minutes. Swap roles with a different domain. The listener rejects a check that cannot distinguish between the competing explanations and asks for a more informative observation.
  5. 8–10 minutes. Each person gives a thirty-second answer. The partner repeats the boundary and next step. Keep one sentence to use when a real interview genuinely exceeds your experience.

Self-check and answer key

For the fictional retry question, Noura does not claim queue expertise or declare the retry safe. Her proposed check investigates duplicate side effects and connects the observation to a retry decision. Reading documentation alone may not establish how this application behaves; testing alone may not describe every guarantee. Your answer passes when the listener can distinguish known experience, proposed reasoning, and unresolved uncertainty. If your check would produce the same result under both competing explanations, it is not yet useful enough. Revise the check rather than adding more confidence words.

Next attempt: Repeat the exercise with a question where the correct first step is to ask for missing context. “Does the job only calculate a report, or does it also change an external account?” can be more useful than proposing an elaborate test immediately. Then practise a knowledge gap with no relevant experience at all. You can say so directly and explain how you would start learning without implying that an interview answer substitutes for training or supervision.
