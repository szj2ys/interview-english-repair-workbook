Explain your work within a team result

Make “I” and “we” refer to different, accurate things.

Goal: Describe a team outcome while giving a listener an accurate map of your decisions, execution, and dependencies.

Start the pair drillUse the worksheetCheck your attempt

A strong team answer does not require replacing every “we” with “I.” That would erase collaboration and can exaggerate ownership. Instead, separate the shared objective from the work you personally performed. A listener should know what you could demonstrate yourself and where you relied on another person’s expertise or authority. This drill repairs unclear subjects and vague verbs. Use an ordinary collaborative task; you do not need a leadership title or a dramatic success to practise it.

Worked example — explicitly fictional

Fictional practice scenario: Tomas helped a small customer-support team introduce a searchable help library. The team’s aim was to make recurring answers easier to find. Tomas says, “Our team built the library. I grouped the repeated questions from the support queue and drafted the first set of troubleshooting pages. A product specialist checked the technical instructions. Our manager decided which pages could be published. After publication, I reviewed the questions that still reached the queue and updated the navigation labels.” This answer identifies his work without claiming that he approved technical accuracy or made the release decision. It also gives credit with enough detail to explain why collaboration mattered.

What goes wrong, and the repair

Weak version: “I built the whole knowledge system and improved support.” If Tomas only wrote and organised pages, that sentence overstates both scope and result. The opposite failure is “We did some documentation together,” which hides his contribution. Replace a vague group verb with two accurate clauses: the team achieved or attempted a shared goal; you performed a named action. Be careful with “led.” It might mean assigning work, setting direction, facilitating meetings, or merely volunteering first. Say which responsibility you actually held. A job title cannot repair an unsupported verb.

Ten-minute pair drill

You need a partner, a timer, and paper or the worksheet below. A partner’s feedback is a comprehension check, not a language score. If practising alone, record and replay each turn, then compare it with the criteria.

  1. 0–2 minutes. A draws three columns on paper: my decisions, my execution, and other people’s work. Choose one genuine project. B asks what evidence or memory supports each entry.
  2. 2–4 minutes. A gives a one-minute explanation with a team objective followed by two personal actions. B writes down who approved, who performed, and who checked the work.
  3. 4–6 minutes. Compare B’s map with A’s columns. Repair any ownership that changed during the telling. If a responsibility was shared, describe the handoff instead of choosing a sole owner.
  4. 6–8 minutes. Swap roles. B answers “What would have happened without your contribution?” A challenges any counterfactual stated as a proven result. Rewrite it as a bounded explanation of the missing task.
  5. 8–10 minutes. Each person repeats one repaired sentence. The partner names a useful follow-up about a personal decision and a different follow-up about the collaboration.

Self-check and answer key

In the fictional example, Tomas owns grouping questions, drafting pages, and revising navigation. The product specialist owns the technical check; the manager owns publication approval. An accurate answer can still use “we” for the library as a team product. Give yourself one check each for a shared objective, a concrete personal action, a named dependency, and an accurate boundary. Missing a boundary is not automatically dishonest, but it is a reason to test the answer with another listener. Do not claim the library reduced support demand unless you have evidence for that separate result.

Next attempt: Practise a project where the result was disappointing. Ownership should remain accurate even when the outcome is uncomfortable. Say what you decided, what information you had, and what you would change. If you cannot reveal names, use roles such as “the reviewer” or “the operations lead.” That keeps the responsibility map understandable without exposing confidential details. The drill is complete when your partner can explain your contribution without giving you the whole team’s work.
